Types of Decision Theory
• Normative decision theory
    • Describes how ideal, rational agents should behave
         • Explains how a rational agent should make decisions
         • Proposes models of preference and rational decision making
         • Preference ordering, maximum expected utility
• Descriptive decision theory
    • Describes how actual agents (humans) really behave
         • Explains how we make decisions
         • Identify potential pitfalls and biases
         • Prospect Theory, Naturalistic decision making
• Prescriptive decision theory
    • Prescribes guidelines for agents to behave rationally
         • Explains how we should make rational decisions
         • Provides guidelines and tools to support decision making
         • Decision analysis and modeling

    Understanding Human Cognition
    • Dual Process Theory
           • Human cognition operates through two distinct systems of thinking
           • These systems influence how we perceive, process information, and make decisions
           • Recognizing these systems helps us understand cognitive biases and improve decision-making
    • System 1 Thinking: (Heuristic Processes)                                                                             Everyday decision making
           • Fast, automatic, effortless, implicit, and emotional
           • Provides speedy, efficient, and often quite accurate responses
                                                                                                                               Complex problem-
    • System 2 Thinking: (Analytic Processes)                                                                             solving and critical thinking
           • Slower, conscious, effortful, explicit, and logical
           • Requires deliberate reasoning, time, and attention

The Availability Heuristic
• Assesses the likelihood or frequency of an event based on how easily
  examples come to mind (readily “available” in memory)
• Examples:
    • Do you fear that crime is increasing after hearing about a few local burglaries?
    • The media just reported a few severe airplane turbulences, do you think flying is more
      dangerous than driving?
    • Would you buy more tickets if you hear that your neighbor has just won the lottery?
    • In an arbitrary English word, is it more likely to find the letter R in the first position or
      in the third position?
• Questions:
    • Why is it a good heuristic? Why is it fallible?

Human Factors                                                                                     16
The Representativeness Heuristic
• Assesses the likelihood of an event or situation based on how
  closely it resembles the existing stereotypes or prototypes.
• Examples:
    • My friend Mo is outgoing, outspoken, adventurous, and hopes to become
      an Air-Force pilot. Do you think Mo is a man or a woman?
    • What is the likely diagnosis of a patient with fever, running nose, cough
      and sore throat?
• Questions:
    • Why is it a good heuristic?
    • Why is it fallible?

The Anchoring and Adjustment Heuristic
• Relies on an initial piece of information (the "anchor") when
  making decisions, and adjust from that starting point to reach
  their final conclusion.
• Other Examples:
    • If you had a good meal at a new restaurant, would you go back again?
    • “Love at first sight,”
• Questions:
    • Why is it a good heuristic?
    • Why is it fallible?

  compensate for them.

Bounded Rationality
• People try to make rational decisions but face limits
• Constraints:
    • Incomplete information
    • Time and cost pressures
    • Limited memory and cognitive capacity
• Humans often satisfice rather than optimize
    • Choose acceptable, not perfect, solutions
    • Stop searching once minimum criteria are met

Human-Guided AI Decision Making
• Human Judgment & Expertise
    • Combine evidence-based reasoning with human values, context, and expertise.
    • Recognize cognitive biases and bounded rationality.
• Applications
    • Prediction, diagnosis, intervention, management, policy, and communication.
    • Human feedback guides AI learning (e.g., RLHF) and reasoning (e.g., process supervision).
• Values & Governance
    • Balance individual, societal, and ethical priorities.
    • Ensure fairness, safety, accountability, and human oversight.
• Improving Decisions
    • Support informed human judgment and mitigate cognitive biases.
    • Strengthen human–AI collaboration through continuous feedback.
