Background: The Elevator Domain
Welcome to the Elevator Challenge, episode II! Your mission, should you choose to accept it, is
to design and implement efficient learning solutions to manage an elevator that serves impatient
office workers who want to reach the ground floor. This seemingly simple scenario presents a large
state space, stochastic passenger arrivals, and long-term consequences for each action.

We study the elevator domain from Assignment 1. The building has 5 floors and 1 elevator. Each
floor can have at most 3 people waiting, and the elevator has a capacity of 10 people. The objective
is to collect passengers from the upper floors and deliver them to the ground floor. New passengers
may arrive at every time step. The elevator can move in its current direction, open its door, or close
its door. Passengers board on upper floors only while the door is open, delivery at the ground floor
is automatic, and the elevator can move only while the door is closed.

The state space is too large for the tabular methods used earlier in the course. In Problem 1, you
implement model-free Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In Problem 2, you extend PPO to a
model-based setting by learning transition and reward models and using them to generate imagined
rollouts.

To understand the domain, explore the provided ElevatorEnv.ipynb, which describes the
state space, action space, and environment interaction. The supplied selfDefinedEnvs folder
contains the RDDL domain and instances used by both problems.

Problem 1: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
In this problem, you will implement Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to solve the Elevator
task. PPO is an actor–critic algorithm that uses a clipped surrogate objective to prevent a policy
update from moving too far from the behavior policy used to collect the rollout [1].

[1] Schulman, J., Wolski, F., Dhariwal, P., Radford, A., & Klimov, O. (2017). Proximal Policy
Optimization Algorithms. arXiv preprint arXiv:1707.06347. https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347.

PPO maintains an actor and a critic. For a state s, the actor defines a categorical policy πθ (a | s)
over the discrete actions, and the critic estimates the state value Vϕ (s). Here, θ and ϕ denote the
actor and critic parameters, respectively.

There are three groups of tasks in Problem 1:

1. Complete the actor–critic network and its forward functions.
2. Given rollout data, compute the temporal-difference residual used by generalized advantage
   estimation.
3. Implement the clipped policy objective, clipped value loss, and combined actor–critic loss.

Problem 2: Model-Based Proximal Policy Optimization (MB-PPO)
Let st be the 13-dimensional elevator state, at the discrete action, rt the reward, st+1 the next state,
and dt the episode-end indicator. In the model-based extension, the agent stores real transitions
(st , at , rt , st+1 , dt ) in a replay buffer. These transitions train a transition model Pbψ (st+1 | st , at )
and a reward model rbω (st ). After a pretraining phase, the learned models generate fixed-length
imagined rollouts, and PPO updates the actor and critic using those rollouts.

The transition model respects the structure of the elevator state. The number of people waiting
on each of the four upper floors is stochastic and is represented by a four-class categorical head.
The number of people in the elevator uses 11 classes, corresponding to counts 0 through 10. The
direction and door components use binary heads, and the elevator location uses a five-class head
whose prediction is converted to a one-hot vector. The ground-floor waiting count is always zero.

There are seven model-learning and model-based training tasks:

1. Define the eight prediction heads of the learned transition model.
2. Concatenate the state and action encodings and complete the transition model’s forward pass.
3. Add valid transitions collected from the real vectorized environments to the replay buffer.
4. Start learned-model training only after the replay buffer contains a complete model mini-batch.
5. Compute a cross-entropy loss for every transition-model output. The supplied reward-model
   loss remains a mean-squared error.
6. Start PPO training in the learned model only after the model-pretraining phase has ended.
7. Construct each predicted next state by sampling the stochastic counts, taking the most likely
   deterministic classes, and converting the predicted elevator floor to one-hot form.

   Solution:


   1. Use four four-class waiting-count heads, one 11-class passenger-count head, two binary

   2. Encode st and at separately, concatenate the two 32-dimensional encodings, and pass the

   3. Insert one transition for each valid vector-environment index. Do not insert the artificial reset
