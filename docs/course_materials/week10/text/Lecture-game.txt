Game Theory                                                                               9
Notes: Main Observations
• Rational ≠ Optimal
• Nash Equilibrium
    • A stable outcome: no player can do better by changing their move alone.
    • → For Almo & Bobo: (Keep, Keep) is the NE with payoffs (+1, +1)
    • → But it’s not the best outcome overall.
• Social Optimum
    • The outcome that maximizes total welfare (sum of both players’ payoffs).
    • → Here, (Share, Share) gives (+3, +3) — total welfare = 6
    • → NE gives total welfare = 2 — a big drop.
• Inefficiency of Equilibrium
    • The NE is Pareto inefficient — both players could do better by cooperating.
• This gap is the Price of Anarchy:
    • the loss in total welfare when players act rationally but selfishly.

• Main observation: Rational ≠ Best for All

Game Strategies & Solutions
• Strategy:
    • Plan of action — pure or mixed.
• Outcome:
    • Payoffs from players’ chosen strategies.
• Solution concept: predicts stable or efficient outcomes.
    • Equilibrium asks whether players have incentives to deviate.
    • Efficiency asks whether the resulting outcome could be collectively improved.
    • Nash equilibrium: stable — no one gains by deviating alone.
    • Pareto optimum: efficient — no one can be better off without hurting another.

Best Response and Equilibria
• Best response:
    • Best response: strategy that maximizes a player’s payoff given others’ choices.
    • A player’s best move, given others’ strategies.
    • Intuition: “If they play 𝑠$! , what’s my best reply?”
• Nash Equilibrium
    • Each player’s strategy is a best response to the others.
    • No one can gain by deviating alone.
    • A Nash equilibrium is a stable outcome
    • A stable outcome is one where no single player can benefit by changing their decision
      alone, assuming everyone else keeps theirs the same - No one gains by deviating alone

Pareto Optimality
• Pareto-optimal:
    • An outcome where no player can be made better off without making
      another worse off.
       → A Pareto-optimal outcome is called efficient.
• Efficient outcome:
    • Uses opportunities so that no further gains are possible without harming
      someone.
        → No one can improve without hurting another.
• Key idea:
    • Stability (Nash) does not imply efficiency (Pareto)!

Minimax Theorem (von Neumann)
• Intuition:
    • In zero-sum games, each player guards against the worst-case.
    • Player 1: maximizes their minimum payoff (maximin).
    • Player 2: minimizes the maximum payoff Player 1 can get (minimax).
    • The minimax and maximin values are equal — the game’s value (𝑣)
        • At equilibrium, Player 1 gets 𝑣 and Player 2 gets −𝑣 (zero-sum)
    • Both players have optimal mixed strategies that guarantee this value.
    • These strategies are mutual best responses → a Nash equilibrium.
• Note:
    • Minimax = Nash equilibrium in mixed-strategy zero-sum games
      — a powerful and unique result!

• Two-Finger Morra: Insights
    • Canonical zero-sum game — one player’s gain equals the other’s loss.
    • No pure equilibrium → players must mix to stay unpredictable.
• Equilibrium:
    • Maximin/minimax solution = mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium
    • Both randomize — one: 7/12, two: 5/12 → stable, mutual best responses.

Complexity of Computing Nash Equilibrium
• Every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium.
    • For 2-player zero-sum games, Nash equilibria can be found efficiently.
• Recall: Question:
    • Is there an efficient algorithm for computing a Nash equilibrium in general?


• Finding a Nash equilibrium in general is computationally intractable:
    • It is PPAD-complete1.
    • No efficient algorithm is known for the general case.
    • Computationally hard even for 2-player games.

Summary: Key Game Concepts
• Key Game Concepts:
    • Normal Form: Matrix showing players and payoffs.
    • Extensive Form: Decision tree of sequential moves.
    • Equilibrium: No player can improve their outcome by changing strategy.
    • Pareto Optimality: No player can be better off without making another worse off.
• Focus:
    • Non-Cooperative & Single Move Games
    • Zero-Sum Games (ZSGs)
• Strategies:
    • Pure Strategy: Consistent, deterministic action
        In ZSGs, one player’s gain equals the other’s loss.
    • Mixed Strategy: Randomized actions to remain unpredictable
        In adversarial ZSGs, used to optimize payoffs

Why Study Game Theory?
• Game theory assumes ideal rational players
    • Real decisions often involve bounded rationality — emotion, bias, or uncertainty.
• Still, these models reveal the strategic logic behind real behavior:
    • Prisoner’s Dilemma: self-interest vs. cooperation
    • Chicken: risk, commitment, and conflict
    • Matching Pennies & Morra: unpredictability and staying unexploitable
    • Zero-sum games: optimal responses in pure competition
• Objective:
    • Studying these games sharpens our ability to anticipate incentives, weigh risks
      and trade-offs, and make better decisions — even in the real world.
