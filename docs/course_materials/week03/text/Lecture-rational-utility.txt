Rational Decision Making
• Context:
     • Decision making under uncertainty, in episodic and partially observable environments.
• Core Idea:
     A rational agent combines:
     • Beliefs – information about the world
     • Preferences – values or goals
     • A decision process – selects actions that maximize expected benefit or minimize cost,
       within resource limits
• Types of Rationality:
     • Substantive (Ends-focused): Achieving desirable outcomes
     • Procedural (Mean-focused): Using a coherent, justifiable method
     • Meta-level: Deciding how to decide (bounded rationality, meta-reasoning)

The BDI Model in Agentic AI
• Belief–Desire–Intention (BDI) Framework:
     • Beliefs: The agent’s model of the world
     • Desires: Goals or preferred outcomes
     • Intentions: Committed plans or actions
• In Agentic AI Systems (e.g., LLM agents):
     BDI provides a natural model for rational behavior:
     • Beliefs inform decision-making
     • Desires guide prioritization
     • Intentions anchor planning and execution
     Modern use: Characterizes AI agents that combine reasoning, planning, and tool use.

   • Origin: Daniel Bernoulli (1738) — measuring risk with

     strategic interaction among agents.
   • Scope: Multi-agent decision making, where outcomes
     depend on all players' actions.

Axioms of Rational Preferences
• Utility Theory:
     • Explains how preferences over lotteries relate to preferences over the
       underlying states.
• Key Idea:
     • Preferences must satisfy certain rationality conditions.
     • If any axiom is violated, the agent can behave irrationally in some situations.
• Six axioms (or conditions of rationality):
     • A1: Orderability (Completeness)       A4: Substitutability (Independence)
     • A2: Transitivity                      A5: Monotonicity
     • A3: Continuity                        A6: Decomposibility

Notes: Calculating Risk Premium
• Definition:
     • Risk Premium = Expected Value (EV) – Certainty Equivalent (CE)
     • EU(Lottery) = U(CE)
• Steps to Calculate:
     • Compute Expected Utility (EU) of the lottery.
     • Find CE: the value whose utility equals the EU.
     • Compute EV of the lottery (e.g., in money, time, health units).
     • Calculate: Risk Premium = EV − CE
• Notes:
     • EV = Expected Value in the original units (e.g., dollars, years, QALYs).
     • For monetary outcomes, EV is called EMV.
     • Utility scale must reflect preferences between best and worst outcomes.
     • CE and PE (Probability Equivalent) methods can be used for utility assessment.

• Preference & Utility
     • Preferences: 𝐴 ≻ 𝐵 (preferred), 𝐴 ∼ 𝐵 (indifferent)
     • Utility function 𝑈 𝑠 : assigns numbers to outcomes, capturing ranking and risk attitude
     • Subjective & personal: not directly comparable across individuals
     • Ordinal scale: numbers express order, not strength of preference
• MEU Principle
     • Rational choice = pick action with Maximum Expected Utility (MEU)
     • Certainty Equivalent (CE): the sure value equal in utility to a lottery
     • EU = utility scale; CE = real-world scale
• Caveats
     • Utility is non-linear; not simply additive 𝑈(𝑎 + 𝑏) ≠ 𝑈(𝑎) + 𝑈(𝑏)
     • Utilities must be assessed at outcome endpoints
     • Multi-attribute and uncertain cases need more advanced models
